Twenty-one years in capital project delivery has convinced me of one thing that surprises engineers and is obvious to operators: **the things that actually go wrong on major investments are rarely about engineering.**

The technical work, by the time experienced engineers are doing it, is usually fine. The problems show up somewhere else.

## Three structural failures I see repeatedly

**Unclear ownership.** A package gets released to procurement with three open questions and four pending decisions. No single person is accountable for closing them. The vendor cannot proceed. Weeks pass. By the time someone notices, fabrication is delayed and the schedule has slipped. Nobody made a mistake in their lane — the lanes themselves were not drawn.

**Misaligned business and engineering expectations.** Operations wants maximum design maturity before committing. The project team wants decisions locked in to protect the schedule. Procurement needs technical data to engage vendors. All three are reasonable. None of them know what the others need. By the time the conflict surfaces in a steering committee, it is already a crisis.

**Teams without the confidence to make decisions early enough.** Engineers defer decisions upward because nobody has explicitly told them they own the call. They escalate, the senior person delays because they want input from another stakeholder, the chain extends, and a decision that should have taken a day takes three weeks. The engineering itself was always sound. The system around it was not.

## The pattern

In every case, fixing the technical work would not have prevented the failure. What was missing was **structure** — the explicit ownership, the shared information, the decision authority, the readiness gates.

This is what good engineering leadership at senior level actually involves. Not deeper technical expertise. Not more reviews. Not more meetings. The work is building the conditions that allow good engineers to perform consistently, regardless of which project they are on.

## What this implies for engineering leadership

If you are leading engineering delivery and you find yourself constantly resolving technical disputes or chasing decisions, the problem is not the engineers. The problem is the system you are operating inside. Build a better one.

The interventions that have mattered most in my own work are not technical contributions. They are structural ones — defining the Equipment Package Unit Manager role to fix accountability gaps in long-lead procurement, building a multidisciplinary design review framework that gives teams a repeatable process, standardising engineering roles across reporting levels so consistency does not depend on individual personalities.

These do not look like engineering work. They are engineering leadership work.

## A test

Next time a delivery problem lands on your desk, ask one question before reaching for the technical solution:

*If we had a stronger structure here — clearer ownership, better shared visibility, earlier decision-making — would this problem have happened at all?*

In my experience, the answer is yes most of the time. And that tells you where the real leverage is.
