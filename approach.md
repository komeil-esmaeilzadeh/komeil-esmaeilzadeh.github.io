How I think about engineering leadership — the frameworks, the principles, and what I have learned about why good engineering organisations succeed and others struggle.

## The core thesis

Most engineering delivery failures are not technical. They are structural. Unclear ownership, misaligned business and engineering expectations, teams without the confidence to make decisions early enough to matter.

Solving them does not require more engineering effort. It requires better structures — and the leadership to build those structures inside organisations that are usually too busy delivering to design them.

This page describes how I do that.

## The AIPEX Framework

AIPEX — short for **A**utomation and **I**nstrumentation **P**erformance & **EX**cellence — is the framework I developed for leading discipline engineering functions inside large capital investment environments. It rests on five pillars.

### 1. Engineering Excellence
Scalable, reusable, portable design — moving from person-dependent expertise to building-block solutions, vendor-neutral standards, and structured design review through the 5C Framework.

### 2. Early Phase Mastery
Right engagement at the right phase — Concept Engineering programmes, structured Site Study Templates, Advanced Work Packaging discipline, and a 5-Level Engineering Readiness Index that prevents cost overruns caused by low maturity entering execution.

### 3. People & Capability
Building owner-engineers, not task executors — through the 3E Framework (Enable → Empower → Expand), OKR-based performance management at function/team/individual level, asynchronous mentorship, and deliberate engineering talent pipelines.

### 4. Partnership Governance
Extracting full value from external engineering investment — through annual resource planning, partner onboarding standards, shared performance indicators, and clear definitions of what is internal versus what is delivered through partner workshare.

### 5. Digital & AI Integration
Neste-approved, pragmatic, outcome-linked — promoting AI tool adoption across the discipline, piloting Document AI for vendor manual processing, advancing model-based design, and quantifying ROACE contribution from advanced process control.

## The 5C Design Review Framework

A structured multidisciplinary design review method I developed and applied across IPEG projects. It moves design review from informal sign-off to a deliberate, repeatable practice:

1. **Clarity of Purpose** — what decision does this review serve
2. **Contextual Inputs** — what does the team need to see
3. **Cross-functional Voices** — who must be in the room
4. **Capture & Confirm** — what was decided, by whom, by when
5. **Continuous Flow** — how this review connects to the next

The Dual-Anchor variant adapts it for EPCM environments with multiple contractors.

## The 3E People Framework

How I think about developing engineers and engineering managers:

- **Enable** — remove the bottlenecks that prevent good people from performing. Budget clarity, decision authority, procurement support, role clarity. Leadership is first about clearing the path.
- **Empower** — give real authority over the scope they own. Coaching, not directing. Asking before telling. Standing behind their decisions even when they would not have been mine.
- **Expand** — connect individual development to a path that extends beyond the current role. Talent pools, succession thinking, structured exposure to scope beyond the comfort zone.

## Principles I keep returning to

**Disagreement is usually about information, not values.** When stakeholders are in conflict, the answer is rarely persuasion. It is shared visibility — giving people the same picture so they can make the trade-offs themselves.

**Leadership style is not a fixed setting.** The same person can be directive with a struggling contractor and gentle with a brilliant introvert who undersells themselves. The skill is reading what someone needs to perform.

**Build the system, not the dependency.** If the team only works when I am in the room, I have failed. The goal is structures, standards, and people who continue to perform consistently regardless of who is leading them.

**Most senior decisions are made on incomplete information.** Waiting for certainty is itself a decision, usually a bad one. The job is to act with integrity in the absence of complete information and to design systems that reduce the uncertainty over time.
